# Legal, Tax, and IP Basics for Solo/Small Indie Devs

> **This is not legal or tax advice.** Everything below is general-principle education compiled from public, sourced material, meant to help a solo or small-team developer ask better questions of a real accountant/lawyer in their own jurisdiction. Business entity law, tax law, and IP law are **jurisdiction-dependent** — most sources here are US-centric (with EU VAT notes where relevant) and will not transfer directly to Canada, the UK, Korea, or elsewhere. Verify everything with a licensed professional before acting on it. This flag applies to every section below, repeated at each section boundary because the stakes (personal liability, tax penalties, lost IP) are high enough that it should not be skimmed past once and forgotten.

---

## 1. Entity basics: sole proprietorship vs LLC vs S-corp (US-centric)

**General principle, not legal/tax advice — verify with a professional in your jurisdiction. This section assumes a US reader; entity types and their tax treatment differ by country.**

### Sole proprietorship
- Default status if you do nothing: if you're a single person selling a game and haven't filed formation paperwork, you're already operating as a sole proprietorship.
- Pros: zero paperwork, zero formation cost, all profit/loss flows straight to your personal tax return.
- Cons: no separation between you and the business — you are personally liable for business debts and legal claims, and it's harder to raise outside investment because investors prefer a formal entity (Kreonit, "Choosing the Right Form of Company for Your Game Development Studio," May 16, 2024, https://kreonit.com/company-and-hiring/choosing-the-right-form-of-company-for-your-game-development-studio/).

### LLC (Limited Liability Company)
- Combines pass-through taxation (profits/losses reported on your personal return, no separate corporate tax return required by default) with liability protection that separates personal assets from business debts and claims (Kreonit, May 16, 2024, same URL as above; Adobe, "Sole Proprietorship vs LLC," https://www.adobe.com/acrobat/business/resources/sole-proprietorship-vs-llc.html, undated).
- Can be single-member (i.e., replaces a sole proprietorship one-for-one while adding the liability shield) in most US states.
- Kreonit's framing, aimed specifically at game studios: an LLC is "the hybrid car of business entities" — it borrows the simplicity of a sole proprietorship and the liability protection of a corporation, which is why it's commonly recommended as the first upgrade for a solo dev who has started earning real revenue or hiring contractors (Kreonit, May 16, 2024).
- Downsides: still subject to self-employment tax on all profit (see Section 2), state-specific filing/renewal fees, and administrative overhead beyond a sole proprietorship.

### S-corp (technically an S-corp is a tax *election*, not a separate entity type — an LLC or corporation can elect S-corp tax treatment)
- Mechanism: instead of paying self-employment tax on 100% of net profit (as a sole proprietor or default LLC does), an S-corp owner becomes a W-2 employee of their own company, pays a "reasonable salary," and the *remaining* profit is distributed as a dividend that is **not** subject to self-employment tax. This salary/distribution split is where the savings come from (OneBridge Accounting, "S Corp Self-Employment Tax Savings: What the Math Actually Looks Like," April 30, 2026, https://onebridgeaccounting.com/blog/s-corp-self-employment-tax-savings/).
- Reported (not independently verified beyond this source) breakeven point: **roughly $80,000 in annual net profit** is where the tax savings commonly start to exceed the added compliance cost; below that, compliance costs often exceed the tax savings (OneBridge Accounting, April 30, 2026). Illustrative numbers from the same source: at $80K profit with a $50K salary, roughly $3,656/year saved; at $120K profit with a $70K salary, roughly $6,245/year; at $150K profit with an $80K salary, roughly $8,954/year. These are illustrative, not guaranteed — actual savings depend on what the IRS would consider a "reasonable salary" for your role, which is itself judgment-dependent and audit-exposed.
- Costs/downsides of S-corp election: mandatory year-round payroll processing, two annual tax filings (Form 1120-S for the business plus your personal 1040) instead of one, federal unemployment tax (reported up to ~$420/year), potentially reduced future Social Security benefits since SE tax also funds those benefits, and roughly $2,000–$4,000/year in added compliance/accounting cost (OneBridge Accounting, April 30, 2026). The same source flags that "highly irregular income" — common for a game studio that earns most of its revenue in a launch-month spike — makes the payroll overhead of an S-corp harder to justify in lean years.
- Corporation (C-corp) more broadly: enables raising capital via stock sales, which matters if you plan to take outside investment, but is subject to double taxation (corporate tax, then personal tax on dividends) and carries more administrative overhead than either sole prop or LLC (Kreonit, May 16, 2024).

### Practical takeaway for a solo/small indie dev
Kreonit's guide (May 16, 2024) frames it as: sole proprietorship for "independent developers starting small," LLC once liability protection or a bit of tax flexibility is worth the added paperwork, and corporation/S-corp once revenue is large and steady enough (commonly cited threshold ~$80K/year net profit, per OneBridge) or outside investment is the goal. This is a **general pattern reported by these sources, not a rule** — the right choice depends on your state, your revenue stability, whether you have contractors or co-founders, and your liability exposure (e.g., multiplayer/UGC games with more legal exposure than a solo singleplayer premium title).

## 2. Tax basics at a principle level (US-centric; VAT section covers EU)

**General principle, not tax advice — verify with a professional, especially before your first quarterly payment or before selling into a new country's market.**

### Self-employment tax
- If you're a sole proprietor, LLC member, or otherwise self-employed, no employer is withholding Social Security/Medicare taxes from your income, so you pay both the "employer" and "employee" halves yourself — commonly cited as **15.3% of net self-employment income** (Johnnemann Nordhagen, "Paying Taxes as a Small Indie," Medium, https://johnnemann.medium.com/paying-taxes-as-a-small-indie-805e4ee6ea41, accessed 2026; date of original post not visible in fetched content — treat as an experienced indie dev's personal account, not an authoritative figure, though the 15.3% rate matches the commonly cited IRS SE tax rate).
- This is *on top of* regular federal/state income tax — it is not a replacement for it.

### Estimated quarterly taxes
- Because nobody is withholding tax from your revenue as it comes in, the IRS expects self-employed people to pre-pay in four installments across the year rather than settling everything at filing time.
- Reported due dates: April 15, June 15, September 15, and January 15 (of the following year) (Paychex, "Quarterly & Estimated Tax Payments 2026," https://www.paychex.com/articles/payroll-taxes/quarterly-taxes; Johnnemann Nordhagen, same Medium post as above — dates match across both sources).
- Threshold that generally triggers the requirement: anticipating you'll owe **$1,000 or more** in tax for the year (Paychex, 2026 article; Nordhagen's post independently states the same $1,000 figure).
- "Safe harbor" rule to avoid underpayment penalties: pay at least 90% of what you'll owe for the current year, **or** 100% of what you owed last year (110% if your prior-year adjusted gross income exceeded $150,000) (Paychex, 2026; corroborated by Nordhagen's post, which states the same three-way test almost verbatim: owe less than $1,000, pay 90% of current year, or pay 100% of last year).
- Penalty for missing/underpaying: reported starting around 0.5% of the amount owed per month, up to a 25% cap (Paychex, 2026); Nordhagen's post separately estimates penalties at roughly 6% of the owed amount — these two figures aren't necessarily contradictory (different penalty mechanisms/time periods can apply) but illustrate that exact penalty math is something to confirm with a professional or the current IRS Form 1040-ES instructions rather than memorize from a blog post.
- Tax applies to **profit, not revenue** — legitimate business expenses (engine licenses, contractor payments, hardware, a home-office deduction, marketing spend, etc.) reduce the taxable amount before the rate is applied. Nordhagen's post gives a concrete illustration: a $50,000 contract with $35,000 in legitimate business costs leaves only $15,000 as taxable income (Nordhagen, same post).
- If you pay contractors: US studios must issue Form 1099 to any contractor paid $600+ in a year (excluding payments routed through third-party processors like PayPal, which follow separate 1099-K rules), due by January 31 of the following year (Nordhagen's post, corroborated by general IRS 1099-NEC filing rules).

### Sales tax / VAT on direct digital sales — and why most solo devs let the platform handle it
- If you sell **directly** (e.g., your own storefront, not through Steam/itch/console stores), you become the party responsible for figuring out sales tax and VAT obligations across every jurisdiction your customers are in — a genuinely complex, ongoing compliance burden.
- The EU's VAT One Stop Shop (OSS) scheme illustrates the complexity a direct seller inherits: since July 1, 2021, the OSS replaced and broadened the older Mini One Stop Shop (MOSS, which existed from January 2015), letting a business register once and file a single quarterly return covering VAT owed to all EU member states, rather than registering separately in each one (European Commission, "The One Stop Shop – VAT e-Commerce," https://vat-one-stop-shop.ec.europa.eu/one-stop-shop_en, accessed 2026). A commonly cited detail: there is a €10,000/year cross-border sales threshold below which you can still apply your home country's VAT rate instead of the buyer's country rate (multiple secondary sources agree on this figure, e.g., Eurocompta, "VAT One-Stop Shop (OSS) in the EU," https://eurocompta.eu/guides/vat-oss/; this specific number should be confirmed against the official europa.eu page before relying on it, since thresholds can change).
- **This is exactly the complexity that platform marketplace-facilitator models absorb for you.** Valve's own Steamworks documentation states that Steam collects VAT/GST/JCT in dozens of countries (reported as 60+, with rates seen ranging roughly 5%–27% across examples like Germany 19%, UK 20%, Japan 10%, Australia 10%) and that "on a periodic basis, Valve remits the tax collected on sales to the appropriate tax authorities" (Valve, Steamworks Documentation, "Taxes FAQ," https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/finance/taxfaq, accessed 2026). Steam pricing is VAT-inclusive in the countries where it collects; for US/Canadian sales tax specifically, tax is added on top of the listed price rather than baked in ("always additive," per the same Steamworks doc). Developer payouts are based on **Net Revenues** (Gross Revenues minus "Applicable Adjustments," which explicitly includes collected taxes) — meaning the VAT/sales tax passes through Valve's books, not yours, and you never have to file it yourself for Steam sales (same source).
- Practical upshot for a solo dev: selling exclusively through marketplace-facilitator platforms (Steam, console stores, itch.io — itch's fee/payout docs did not load for direct verification in this research pass, so treat itch's specific VAT-handling claim as unverified pending a direct check of https://itch.io/docs/creators) meaningfully reduces your tax-compliance surface area compared to running your own storefront. This is a commonly cited reason solo devs avoid direct-sale storefronts even though the platform's revenue cut (Steam's standard 30%, tiered down at revenue thresholds) is a real cost — the alternative is taking on VAT/sales-tax registration and remittance in every market you sell into yourself.
- This is still jurisdiction- and platform-specific: confirm current facilitator status and coverage for whichever platform(s) you actually use, since coverage (which countries, which tax types) can change and isn't necessarily identical across all storefronts.

### R&D tax credits
- Reported as commonly discussed among game studios (Baker Tilly, "How the R&D tax credit can benefit video game developers," https://www.bakertilly.com/insights/rd-tax-credit-for-video-game-developers — could not fetch full text, returned HTTP 403; treat the title/topic as confirmed but content below sourced from IGDA instead).
- IGDA's resource page states: federal R&D credit can recover up to **10%** of qualified research expenses; roughly 39 states offer their own R&D credit programs on top, recovering up to **12%** of expenses in some states (IGDA, "R&D Tax Credit Opportunities for Video Game Developers," article dated February 28, 2022, https://igda.org/resources-archive/rd-tax-credit-opportunities-for-video-game-developers/).
- Startup-specific provision: under the PATH Act, a company can offset **payroll taxes** (not just income tax) up to $500,000/year (raised from $250,000 by the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022, for tax years beginning after 2022-12-31; verified 2026-07-26 at https://www.irs.gov/credits-deductions/research-credit-against-payroll-tax-for-small-businesses) [ORIGINAL TEXT SAID $250,000/year with unused R&D credits if it has less than $5 million in gross receipts in the credit year and five or fewer years of gross receipts — this is specifically relevant to a pre-revenue or early-revenue small studio that owes little/no income tax yet but does have payroll (IGDA, Feb 28, 2022).
- Qualifying activities per IGDA's summary span the full dev cycle: technical feasibility work, engine/tooling work, gameplay systems, UI/sound/character design work, alpha/beta testing, and deployment optimization — essentially most non-routine engineering and design work counts, not just "science."
- Qualifying expenses: employee wages for developers/designers/QA/engineers, non-depreciable supplies (dev kits, VR headsets, test devices), cloud compute (AWS/Azure explicitly named as meeting IRS criteria), and a portion (65%, per IGDA) of contracted-out development work.
- Retroactive claims: businesses can look back three years and claim refunds for R&D credits not claimed in prior returns (IGDA, Feb 28, 2022).
- Caveat: this is one of the more jurisdiction- and situation-specific areas here — whether your specific work qualifies, and whether the credit is worth the accounting cost of substantiating it, is exactly the kind of question to bring to a CPA rather than infer from a blog summary.

## 3. IP basics: copyright vs trademark as applied to games

**General principle, not legal advice — verify with a professional, and note that copyright/trademark mechanics differ by country even though the copyright-is-automatic / trademark-requires-registration-to-be-strong pattern is broadly similar across many jurisdictions.**

### What copyright automatically covers
- Copyright protection attaches **automatically, the moment you create the work** — no registration is required for the protection to exist. This is confirmed by an interview with game/IP lawyer Jacob Vela (freakingcoolindies.com, "Game Developer Legal Foundations: Copyright, Trademark, and Contract Essentials with indie Lawyer Jacob Vela," https://freakingcoolindies.com/11-2/, accessed 2026): copyright covers art, code, sound, music, story, and characters as they are actually expressed.
- Critical limitation Vela emphasizes: copyright protects **specific expression**, not underlying ideas or mechanics. His example: "The idea of platforming isn't protectable, but Super Mario Brothers' specific expression is" — i.e., you can't copyright "a battle royale game" or "a deckbuilder," only your specific art, code, and narrative content.
- **Registration is still practically important even though it's not required for protection to exist**: per Vela, registering with the US Copyright Office is "cheap and easy to do yourself" and is what actually enables litigation and DMCA-style takedown rights in practice. Current official fee for the cheapest tier (single author, single work, not for hire, filed online) is **$45**; the standard online application fee is **$65**; paper filing is **$125** (US Copyright Office, "Fees," https://www.copyright.gov/about/fees.html, accessed 2026 — current fee schedule).
- DMCA enforcement is itself a documented, IGDA-covered topic: "Copyright Enforcement Through the U.S. Digital Millennium Copyright Act" (IGDA Resources Archive, 2016, https://igda.org/resources-archive/copyright-enforcement-through-the-u-s-digital-millennium-copyright-act-2016/) — relevant if you ever need to take down a pirated build or a clone asserting your art/code was copied.

### What needs active registration/enforcement: trademark
- Trademark covers **branding elements** distinct from the creative work itself: your game's title, your studio's name, and your logo (freakingcoolindies.com interview with Jacob Vela, accessed 2026; IGDA, "Trademarks – What Game Developers Should Know," whitepaper by Eric Magleby, Forrest A. Jones, Naresh Kilaru, and Anthony D. Del Monaco, published March 29, 2021, https://igda.org/resources-archive/trademarks-what-game-developers-should-know-2021/ — full PDF content wasn't fully extractable in this research pass beyond its framing/scope, but the byline and date are confirmed real IGDA Business & Legal SIG output). IGDA had a separate, earlier "IGDA Business and Legal SIG Position on Trademarks" published in 2014 (https://igda.org/resources-archive/igda-business-and-legal-sig-position-on-trademarks-2014/), indicating this has been a recurring topic of concern for the org for at least a decade.
- Unlike copyright, trademark strength and enforceability depend heavily on **distinctiveness**, commonly explained via the "Abercrombie spectrum" (per Vela's interview): generic terms (e.g., calling your game "Video Game") get no protection; descriptive names are weak and need to earn "secondary meaning" through market recognition; suggestive names (Vela's examples: Electronic Arts, Street Fighter) are registerable; arbitrary names (Valve, Bungie — real words used in an unrelated context) are strong; fanciful/invented names (Pokemon, Sega) are strongest. Practical advice from Vela: aim for arbitrary or fanciful names, and always search the USPTO database before committing to a title.
- Registration specifics (Mekiplaw, "How to Trademark a Game: The Ultimate Guide," https://www.mekiplaw.com/how-to-trademark-a-game-the-ultimate-guide/, accessed 2026 — filing-fee figures were not present in the fetched content, so exact USPTO cost could not be confirmed in this pass): the USPTO process commonly takes **about 12 months**; you can file before release with an "intent to use" application, but the three-year limit runs from the **Notice of Allowance**, not from your release date — the Statement of Use is due within three years of the NOA issuance date [corrected 2026-07-26 against https://www.uspto.gov/trademarks/apply/intent-use-itu-forms]; trademark applications reportedly face examiner rejection on first pass **over 80% of the time**, requiring a response; using a trademark attorney is reported to increase success odds by "over 50%" (unverified beyond this single source — treat as a marketing claim from a law-adjacent guide rather than a rigorously sourced statistic). Trademark registration is explicitly **not mandatory** — without it you have no exclusive federal rights and can't easily stop a copycat from using an identical or confusingly similar name, but you also save the cost/time if your title isn't commercially significant enough to be worth defending.
- Practical decision framing for an indie dev: because copyright protects your actual creative content automatically and for free, and trademark only protects the *name/branding* and requires active registration/enforcement effort to have teeth, the realistic decision point is usually "is my game's title/studio name valuable enough as a brand (sequels planned, merch planned, franchise ambitions) to be worth the ~12-month registration process and the ongoing burden of policing it against confusingly similar names?" A one-off game with no sequel plans is a much weaker case for trademarking than a title intended to become a franchise or a studio name intended to be reused across multiple games.

### Design patents, utility patents, trade secrets (adjacent, less commonly relevant to solo devs)
- IGDA's resource archive separately covers "Design Patents – What Game Developers Should Know" (2020) and "Utility Patents – What Game Developers Should Know" (2021), plus "Trade Secrets: What Game Developers Should Know" (2021) (https://igda.org/resources-archive/, listing accessed 2026). These exist but are generally lower-priority for a solo/small indie dev than copyright/trademark basics — patents are expensive and slow to obtain and are more relevant to novel technical innovations (e.g., a genuinely new rendering technique) than to game design or content, which is the more common indie IP concern.

## 4. Contractor IP assignment: why it matters and what a basic clause needs

**General principle, not legal advice — verify any actual contract language with a lawyer, especially for international contractors where local moral-rights law can override a US-style agreement.**

### The default-ownership trap
- **By default, an independent contractor owns the IP they create — even work you paid them to make for your game** — unless a written agreement explicitly transfers those rights to you (Trademarkia, "Who Owns Intellectual Property Created by Independent Contractors?," https://www.trademarkia.com/news/legal-info/who-owns-ip-created-by-contractors/, accessed 2026; corroborated by Revision Legal's analysis below).
- Jacob Vela's interview makes the same point concretely for a common indie scenario: "When this group of friends makes a game together without any formal agreement, who owns the intellectual property by default? The answer is all of them" — i.e., informal collaboration among friends without contracts creates *joint, ambiguous* ownership, not clean ownership by whoever's "in charge." His broader framing: "Contracts aren't about trust; they're about clarity and preventing misunderstandings that destroy partnerships" (freakingcoolindies.com, accessed 2026).

### Why "work for hire" language alone isn't enough
- US copyright law (17 U.S.C. § 101) only allows independent-contractor work to automatically qualify as "work made for hire" if it falls into one of **nine specific statutory categories** — and custom game code/art commissioned from a freelancer frequently does **not** fall cleanly into any of them (Revision Legal, "Work for Hire Agreements for Mobile Game Developers," published January 11, 2016, https://revisionlegal.com/corporate/revision-legal/work-for-hire-mobile-game-developers/).
- Documented legal risk: courts have ruled **against** the hiring party in cases where a contract used "work for hire" language for work that didn't fit the nine categories, and the contract lacked a separate, explicit assignment clause as a fallback — meaning the contractor could end up retaining rights despite the studio believing it had "work for hire" coverage (Revision Legal, Jan 11, 2016).
- Fallback/backup assignment clause is the standard fix. Revision Legal's recommended structure (Jan 11, 2016): the clause should state that (1) to the extent the work legally qualifies as work-for-hire under §101, it is a work made for hire; and (2) to the extent it does *not* so qualify, the contractor irrevocably assigns all right, title, and interest in the work to the studio. Including both clauses means you're covered whether or not the "work for hire" designation legally holds up.
- International complication: many countries recognize **inalienable "moral rights"** for creators (e.g., right of attribution, right to object to derogatory treatment of the work) that can persist even after a US-style copyright assignment, meaning a contractor in a moral-rights jurisdiction could still assert certain rights in their home country regardless of what a US contract says (Revision Legal, Jan 11, 2016). This is a real risk for the common scenario of hiring a freelance artist or composer overseas.

### What a basic assignment clause needs to cover, in practice
Per Revision Legal (Jan 11, 2016) and general contractor-IP guidance (Trademarkia; xsolla.com, "5 contracts every game developer needs," accessed 2026, cited by search-result title/URL only — full content not independently fetched in this pass):
1. **Explicit assignment language**, not just "work for hire" — as described above.
2. **Precise deliverable definitions** — vague deliverable descriptions create room to argue over what was actually assigned. Name specific assets, files, or systems.
3. **Coverage of derivative works** — the studio should own the right to modify, extend, and create sequels/expansions using the contracted work, not just the original delivered asset.
4. **Pre-existing IP carve-out** — a clause distinguishing IP the contractor already owned before the engagement (e.g., a sound library, a proprietary shader, an asset-store package) from newly created, assignable work — otherwise ownership of pre-existing tools/assets used in the deliverable can become disputed.
5. **Moral rights waiver**, especially for international contractors, to reduce (though not always fully eliminate) the risk described above.
6. **Category-specific coverage**: source code, art, audio, and — increasingly relevant for live-service or monetized indie titles — monetization systems and any LiveOps-related IP should be named separately rather than assumed to be covered by one blanket clause (Revision Legal, Jan 11, 2016, paraphrased recommendation).

### Practical takeaway
Vela's interview and Revision Legal's analysis converge on the same operational advice: **put a contract in place before work starts, not after**, and make sure it contains an explicit assignment clause rather than relying on "work for hire" language alone — this is one of the cheapest risk-reduction steps a solo/small studio can take relative to the cost of a later ownership dispute with a former contractor.
