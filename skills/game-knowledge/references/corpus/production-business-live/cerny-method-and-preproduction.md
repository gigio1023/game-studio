# Mark Cerny's Method and Pre-Production Doctrine

## What "the Method" is

Mark Cerny — game designer/producer, credits include Marble Madness, Crash
Bandicoot, Spyro the Dragon, Jak and Daxter, and later lead architect of the
PS4 and PS5 — developed and publicly named a production methodology he calls
simply "Method," drawn from roughly 20 years of experience at the time,
including work with Naughty Dog and Insomniac Games and other Sony first-party
studios (GameDeveloper.com/Gamasutra, Sep 6, 2002; Iterative/Joe Mahoney
summary, undated).

Cerny first presented Method publicly at the inaugural D.I.C.E. Summit in
2002 (Wikipedia "Mark Cerny," accessed 2026; corroborated by the
GameDeveloper.com article dated Sep 6, 2002, which covers the same talk).
The framing problem he opens with: studios routinely spend most of a
project's budget and schedule *before* they actually know what the game is,
discovering the design only deep into what should have been production. In
his words, paraphrased in coverage of the talk: **"You will spend a million
dollars before you know what your game is"** (Mobilefreetoplay.com, "Why
Games Fail: Pre-Production," undated, summarizing Cerny's talk — treat this
exact wording as a paraphrase/secondary-source quote, not a verified verbatim
transcript quote).

## The core split: pre-production vs. production are different jobs

Method's central move is to treat pre-production and production as two
distinct disciplines with two different outputs — a *game design* versus a
*game* — that require different working styles:

- **Pre-production is for learning and prototyping.** Its job is to answer
  the question "is this game good, and do we know how to build it," as
  cheaply as possible.
- **Production is for putting that learning into action** — i.e.,
  manufacturing content at scale once the recipe is proven.

Cerny is quoted (via secondary summary) as saying **"production is easy, but
pre-production is hard"** (Iterative/Joe Mahoney, "Mark Cerny describes the
'Method,'" undated summary of the 2002 DICE talk). The GameDeveloper.com
coverage frames the same point operationally: **"Pre-production is about
concentrating all that work that gets tossed out into the very first few
months"** (GameDeveloper.com/Gamasutra, Sep 6, 2002) — i.e., pre-production is
deliberately where you pay for the churn and thrown-away art/animation/code
that would otherwise happen mid-production at far higher cost, because a team
built content before establishing what the game actually was.

## Budget and time allocation

Per the same Sep 6, 2002 GameDeveloper.com/Gamasutra report on Cerny's talk:

- Cerny recommended allocating **roughly 30% of total project budget to
  pre-production** — on the larger projects of that era, on the order of $1
  million.
- The stated logic: this lets a publisher **risk 30% of the budget rather
  than 100%** when greenlighting a new concept, because the pre-production
  phase is explicitly a go/no-go gate, not committed production spend.
- Cerny is reported to have argued that games projected to sell under
  **50,000 units** "should not, by and large, be made" — i.e., Method is also
  a portfolio-level risk filter for the publisher, not just a per-project
  process tweak. (GameDeveloper.com/Gamasutra, Sep 6, 2002.)

A later secondary source specific to free-to-play mobile production (which
adapts Method-style thinking to a different genre/business model) gives a
different, more genre-specific range: **pre-production spanning 3 months (for
a team already experienced in the target genre) up to 1+ year (for a team
exploring an unfamiliar genre)** (Mobilefreetoplay.com, "Why Games Fail:
Pre-Production," undated). This is a downstream adaptation of Cerny's
doctrine to F2P mobile, not Cerny's own stated figure for the console/retail
projects his 2002 talk was about — treat the 3-months-to-1-year figure as
genre-specific commentary, not as Cerny's own number.

**Caveat on precision:** none of the secondary sources found reproduce a
citable primary transcript or slide deck with exact, unambiguous duration
figures tied to specific shipped Sony titles (e.g., exact pre-production
month-counts for Uncharted or Knack). The 30%-of-budget figure is the most
consistently repeated concrete number across sources and traces to the 2002
GameDeveloper.com/Gamasutra report specifically.

## "Publishable first playable" — the doctrine's centerpiece

The output pre-production is supposed to produce, under Method, is not a
throwaway tech demo or a grey-boxed proof of concept — it is a **"publishable
first playable"**: a single, small, but genuinely polished slice of the game
that could, in principle, be shipped as-is (hence "publishable"), even though
it is not content-complete.

- It does **not** need to be content-complete — it can be one level, one
  encounter, one loop.
- It **does** need to be finished-quality: final-bar art, final-bar feel,
  final-bar UI in that one slice — because the entire point is to test
  whether *this specific game, at final quality*, is fun and sellable, not
  whether a rough approximation of it might eventually be fun.
- It is explicitly built to be used in **playtesting with real, external
  players** — not just internal team judgment — to determine whether the
  concept excites people (VGC "Who is Mark Cerny," accessed 2026; Iterative
  summary, undated).
- The decision rule is binary and unsentimental: if the first playable does
  not excite players, **the project should be abandoned or reworked before
  further money is spent**, rather than pushed into production on hope
  (VGC, accessed 2026; GameDeveloper.com/Gamasutra, Sep 6, 2002).

One secondary account paraphrases Cerny's own description of the pattern once
the first playable succeeds: you iterate on a single level until it "showed
exactly the game you were trying to make, and once you had the level, then
you make 30 of them" (paraphrase reported in a WebSearch synthesis of
Wikipedia/press coverage; **primary quote not independently verified in this
research pass — treat as commonly repeated paraphrase, not confirmed
verbatim**). The idea it conveys — that production is mechanical repetition
of a *proven* recipe, done at scale, once pre-production has nailed the
recipe — is consistent with the rest of the sourced material above, even
where the exact wording isn't nailed down.

Cerny is also reported to run a "**pre-post-mortem**" at the end of
pre-production: before greenlighting full production, the team explicitly
writes down (a) what must be exceptional for the game to succeed, (b) what
could kill the project if mishandled, and (c) which assumptions still need
confirming (Mobilefreetoplay.com, undated, summarizing Method's later
articulations). This is functionally a structured risk register gating the
pre-production → production transition.

## The broader industry doctrine: "the game is proven, now scale it"

Cerny's Method is the most publicly attributed and named version of a split
that is widely treated as industry-standard practice, independent of Cerny
specifically:

- **Pre-production** = planning phase focused on concept development,
  design-document iteration, prototyping, and risk reduction; per Wikipedia's
  "Video game development" article (accessed 2026, citing secondary
  industry sources), pre-production is "usually not funded by a publisher" in
  the traditional milestone-payment structure — it is speculative,
  self-funded, or seed-funded work the studio does before a contract exists.
- **Production** = "the main stage of development when assets and source
  code for the game are produced," beginning once "the project is fully
  staffed" (Wikipedia "Video game development," accessed 2026).

The transition point is conventionally understood industry-wide as: *the
game is proven — the design risk has been retired — now the job is to scale
known-good content production*, as opposed to continuing to search for what
the game is. This mirrors Cerny's framing almost exactly, which is part of
why Method is widely cited as having shaped, or at least crisply named, the
mainstream pre-production/production split still taught in the industry today
(Iterative/Joe Mahoney summary, undated, explicitly notes Method emerged
"around the same time as the Agile Manifesto" [2001] but describes it as
"more concrete" than the Manifesto's general principles).

## Risk-first prototyping: prototype the riskiest thing first

A closely related but distinct practice, documented across several named
practitioners, is choosing *what to prototype first* by risk rather than by
ease or excitement:

- **Daniel Cook ("Danc"), Lostgarden**, "Managing Game Design Risk, Part I"
  (Apr 3, 2006): frames game development risk in three interacting
  categories — **financial risk** (will it be profitable), **requirements
  risk** (are we building the right product, correct scope, correct market
  fit), and **execution risk** (can the team actually deliver it — people,
  tech, schedule, quality). Cook's key warning, directly relevant to
  over-scoping: optimizing for a single risk category (e.g., adding more
  features to reduce requirements/design risk) routinely creates *worse*
  execution risk through added team communication overhead, technical
  complexity, and schedule uncertainty — cutting scope isn't free, but
  neither is padding it. Cook previews a follow-up essay on
  "prototype-driven development" aimed at "finding the fun earlier" by
  embracing change instead of specifying it away up front.

- **Chris Hecker**, "Advanced Prototyping" (GDC talk, exact year not
  independently re-verified in this pass; referenced as a foundational
  influence by Joseph Kim, see below) — cited secondhand as an early,
  influential articulation of prototyping strategy in games. **[Primary talk
  not directly reviewed in this research pass; flagged via a secondary
  source's citation, not independently confirmed.]**

- **Joseph Kim**, "'Key Risks' Based Game Pre-production" (gamemakers.com,
  Jan 11, 2017): proposes a **Key Risks Table (KRT)** — before prototyping
  gameplay, list the top 3–4 risks that could sink the project (e.g., netcode
  viability, IP/licensing constraints, monetization viability for a mobile
  title), then design a *targeted* prototype for each risk — which may be a
  competitive analysis, a wireframe, or a software mockup, not necessarily a
  playable gameplay build. Kim explicitly credits designer Alex Mandryka and
  Chris Hecker's "Advanced Prototyping" talk as conceptual predecessors.
  States pre-production has three objectives: (1) a clear design vision, (2)
  an estimate of financial characteristics, and (3) identifying/mitigating
  the game's key risks before major spend.

- **Paul Tozour**, "Managing Risk in Video Game Development"
  (GameDeveloper.com/Gamasutra, May 3, 2013): advocates **Discovery-Driven
  Planning (DDP)** — a methodology (adapted from general management
  literature, not games-specific in origin) that separates known facts from
  assumptions and prioritizes testing the *most consequential, least-certain*
  assumptions earliest and cheapest, via structured "checkpoints." Tozour
  argues the industry frequently substitutes "instinct or guesswork" for
  structured risk assessment. Illustrates with a fictional mobile strategy
  title, "City Conquest" — explicitly a worked hypothetical, not a real
  shipped case study.

- **Aakash Gupta**, "Prototype-Type Games" (aakashg.com, May 1, 2026):
  reframes prototypes as "structured experiments that answer a specific
  business question" and gives a decision matrix matching prototype *type* to
  risk *type* — paper prototypes for testing rules/comprehension, clickables
  for UI-flow/usability risk, greyboxes for feel/pacing/spatial-mechanic
  risk, vertical slices for proving feasibility to leadership. Central
  quote: **"Match the prototype to the risk, not to the enthusiasm level of
  the team."** No named studios or shipped titles are cited as case studies
  in this piece — it is a general framework article.

- **God of War (2018) single-shot camera** as a real, named example of a
  studio betting pre-production effort on its riskiest creative idea: director
  Cory Barlog described the unbroken-camera concept as something he "had to
  take it on faith" internally because there was no precedent to point to —
  "I can't cite something else" — and reported that roughly **40% of the
  team** only understood why the choice mattered *after* playing the finished
  game (GameDeveloper.com, "Barlog: God of War's 'single shot' camera trick
  was a tough sell for devs," accessed 2026). The finished camera uses
  "six to eight tricks" to maintain the unbroken-shot illusion. **Caveat:**
  this source documents the camera as a hard, faith-based creative sell and a
  structurally risky bet, but it does **not** explicitly frame the camera
  work as "the riskiest mechanic, prototyped first" in Cerny-Method terms —
  that connection is this document's inference, not a claim made in the
  source. No concrete prototyping timeline or de-risking methodology is given
  in the source.

## Summary judgment on source quality

- **High confidence, well-sourced:** the existence and 2002 DICE origin of
  Method; the pre-production/production split as "different jobs, different
  outputs"; the ~30%-of-budget pre-production allocation figure; the
  publishable-first-playable concept and its playtesting/go-no-go function;
  the general industry acceptance of "pre-production de-risks, production
  scales" as doctrine.
- **Medium confidence / paraphrase-only:** the exact "$1 million," "you will
  spend a million dollars before you know what your game is," and "make 30
  of them" quotes — these appear only in secondary summaries, not verified
  primary transcripts, in this research pass.
- **Folklore / not independently verified:** any claim that Method
  prescribes an exact universal pre-production duration in months for
  console/retail AAA projects — no source found gives Cerny's own number for
  this; the 3-month–to–1-year figure found is a mobile/F2P-specific
  adaptation, not Cerny's own stated figure.
