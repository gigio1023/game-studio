## 1. Patch cadence practice

### The widely repeated pattern: hotfix fast, then decline
The most consistently repeated pattern across multiple sources (none individually authoritative, but converging) is: ship emergency hotfixes for crashes/save-corruption/game-breaking bugs within **24–48 hours** of discovery, then settle into a slower cadence of scheduled patches — weekly or biweekly — for less urgent fixes and features, with the fix-to-feature ratio shifting over time as stability improves (Bugnet Blog, "Early Access Bug Management Strategies," by Sofia Reyes, Developer Relations, published March 19, 2026, https://bugnet.io/blog/early-access-bug-management-strategies — **explicitly noted by this research pass as containing no citations of named studios or postmortems; presented as generic best-practice guidance**). The same article's more granular (also unsourced) breakdown: month 1 post-launch commonly run roughly 60% bugfix / 40% new-feature work, months 2+ shift toward 50/50, and by month 3+ (once crash-free rate is reportedly above 99%) shift to roughly 40% fixes / 60% features. Treat these ratios as a plausible mental model, not a documented industry standard.

### A concrete, dated, sourced example: Cyberpunk 2077 (AAA, launch-crisis case)
Cyberpunk 2077 launched December 10, 2020 (widely and publicly documented — one of the most reported game launches in the industry). CD Projekt Red's own official hotfix announcement confirms **Hotfix 1.05** went live on PC, Xbox, and PlayStation on **December 19, 2020** (CD Projekt Red, "Hotfix 1.05," cyberpunk.net official news, published/dated 2020-12-19, https://cyberpunk.net/en/news/37166/hotfix-1-05) — i.e., a hotfix roughly nine days after launch, consistent with the general "hotfix within days, not months, for a launch-blocking crisis" pattern even at AAA scale. Broader (less rigorously sourced) characterization of the following months from an industry-commentary blog: "major patches came out monthly (at best)" for Cyberpunk 2077 in the months after launch, versus a same-article claim that solo-developer Stardew Valley saw "bugs squashed within hours or days" (Fablesy.com, "How Patch Cycles Vary Between Indie and AAA Titles," by Aurora Sharpe, published February 13, 2026, https://www.fablesy.com/article/how-patch-cycles-vary-between-indie-and-aaa-titles — **this article discloses that its images were AI-generated and cites no primary sources for its comparative claims; treat the "monthly at best" and specific day/week ranges as unsourced characterization, not verified fact.** The independently confirmed December 19, 2020 hotfix date above is the only piece of this example verified against an official primary source in this research pass).

### A concrete, dated, sourced example: Stardew Valley (solo dev, long-tail support)
Stardew Valley, developed solo by Eric Barone ("ConcernedApe"), launched February 26, 2016. Wikipedia's article on the game documents that Barone "continued developing Stardew Valley since its official release, incorporating community feedback, fixing bugs, and adding new features" on an ongoing basis for years, not weeks (Wikipedia, "Stardew Valley," accessed 2026, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stardew_Valley). Concretely documented major-update milestones from the same source: a multiplayer beta for Windows in April 2018, full multiplayer launch across PC platforms on August 1, 2018, Nintendo Switch multiplayer in December 2018, and a substantial version 1.6 content update published for PC in March 2024 (with mobile/console following in November 2024). This illustrates a genuinely different cadence *shape* for a runaway-success solo project: instead of "patch heavily for weeks then taper off," Stardew Valley shows a pattern of ongoing bugfixing punctuated by *major* content updates spaced **years** apart — worth noting as an outlier case (exceptional commercial success funding years of continued solo development) rather than a typical small-indie pattern, since most small/solo titles don't have the sales volume to justify multi-year post-launch investment.

### Early Access-specific cadence guidance
A game-marketing-agency source frames a **minimum viable communication cadence during Early Access** as: bi-weekly "mini updates" (small patches or communication touchpoints) plus a monthly "state of the game" post (broader progress report/roadmap discussion), with detailed patch notes accompanying every major release — described as "the minimum dialogue cadence the EA community expects" (Althera Games, "Steam Early Access: Is It the Right Path for Your Indie Game?", published May 1, 2026, https://altheragames.com/en/blog/steam-early-access-guide). The same source recommends keeping a public roadmap's "In Progress" column deliberately short (3–5 items) to visibly signal focus rather than scattered effort. This is a single marketing-agency source, not a cross-studio survey, so treat the specific "bi-weekly + monthly" cadence as one documented recommendation rather than a proven optimum.

### Distinguishing AA/AAA vs. solo-dev cadence
Putting the above together: **AAA cadence** is constrained by console certification/cert-pass requirements (adding days to weeks of lag between a fix being ready and it shipping on console) and larger QA/release pipelines, which is the commonly cited reason AAA "major" patches land on a roughly monthly rhythm even when hotfixes for the most severe issues still ship within days (per the Cyberpunk 2077 example above and the general pattern claimed by Fablesy.com, unsourced). **Solo/small-indie cadence** is typically less constrained by certification lag on PC (Steam patches can ship same-day at the developer's discretion) but is constrained instead by the developer's own bandwidth — meaning the *floor* for hotfix speed can actually be faster for a motivated solo dev on PC, but the *sustained* cadence is more fragile and more dependent on the game's ongoing commercial success continuing to justify the developer's time (per the Stardew Valley long-tail case above).

## 2. Community management: minimal viable practice

### Discord server setup and moderation basics
Two independently useful, real (non-content-mill) sources converge on a similar minimal structure:

**Discord's own official guidance** (Discord, "The Game Developer Playbook, Part One: Getting Started on Discord," by Nelly, published March 17, 2025, https://discord.com/blog/the-game-developer-playbook-part-one-getting-started-on-discord) recommends organizing channels into four category types — read-only info, two-way discussion, staff-only, and dedicated voice rooms for playtests — plus specific channels like `#rules-and-info`, `#announcements`/`#patch-notes`, `#game-discussion`/`#build-feedback`, and a standardized `#report-issue` channel for bug reports. Minimum role setup: one admin/studio role with full access, one restricted playtester role, with the `@everyone` role stripped of default visibility so private content stays private. Recommended baseline moderation: enable **Community** server features (unlocks announcement channels and onboarding tools), set a **Verification Level** requiring email/phone confirmation before posting, use **Rules Screening** so members must acknowledge guidelines before participating, and enable **AutoMod** plus explicit-content filtering. Named moderation bot examples: DynoBot, ProBot, Ticket Tool.

**A real named indie studio's account** (Buddy Sola, Akupara Games, "Dissecting Discord: How to Set Up an Indie Game Discord Server," Medium, published March 10, 2021, https://akuparagames.medium.com/dissecting-discord-how-to-set-up-an-indie-game-discord-server-ce762c7d4dcd) gives a more battle-tested/practitioner view: keep landing-page channels minimal (`#announcements`, `#welcome-rules`, `#introduce-yourself`), add a couple of low-barrier casual channels (Sola's example: `#food`, not `#chew-on-this` — i.e., use clear, legible channel names over cute ones) to give new members something easy to engage with besides the game itself, and separate **broadcast channels** (read-only announcements) from conversational ones. Practical role advice: use an automatically assigned baseline role (Sola's example: `@Gamer`) so you can ping the active community without invoking `@everyone` (which can cause members to mute the server), and prefer role-specific pings over broad ones. Sola's stated community-management practices: respond to messages in real time when possible, keep a professional tone about the company/games while being casual about everything else, and prioritize bug-report conversations happening *in Discord* over parallel channels like forums or email — i.e., Discord becomes the primary support surface, not just a marketing channel.

### Devlog cadence
Concrete, cross-checkable devlog-frequency data was hard to find from a heavyweight named source in this research pass. The most repeated figure across lower-confidence sources: **roughly one substantial devlog (video or long written post) every 1–2 weeks**, supplemented by more frequent (2–3/week) "micro-posts" (GIFs, screenshots, short clips) on social media in between (script2shorts.app, "Game Devlog Content Strategy: What to Post and When," accessed 2026, https://script2shorts.app/blog/game-devlog-content-strategy — **content-mill-style source, no case studies cited; treat as a plausible pattern, not verified data**). This is broadly consistent with the Early Access "bi-weekly mini update + monthly state-of-game post" cadence from Althera Games cited above, suggesting a convergent (if not rigorously proven) community expectation of **roughly biweekly** touchpoints as a floor, both pre- and post-launch.

### How this ties into Steam's algorithmic visibility (primary source: Steamworks docs)
This is the best-sourced part of this file, drawn directly from Valve's own Steamworks Documentation (partner.steamgames.com), current as of this research pass in 2026:

- **Update Visibility Rounds**: a developer-triggered feature (manually started from the app's Marketing admin page) intended for "major updates to your application, add new content, release new DLC, or ship new features." Critically, starting a round **requires selecting a recent community announcement, and that announcement must have been posted within the last 30 days** — meaning a stale news feed directly blocks access to this visibility mechanism (Valve, Steamworks Documentation, "Update Visibility Rounds," https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/marketing/visibility/update_rounds, accessed 2026). Each round "appear[s] to customers that have your game in their library or on their wishlist," runs for up to 30 days or caps at 1 million homepage impressions (whichever comes first), and each product starts with **five** allocated rounds shared across Early Access and full release. Rounds "will typically not show up during major seasonal sale events," so timing an announcement/update around a sale window can waste the visibility opportunity.
- **Wishlist notifications**: Steam automatically emails/notifies wishlisters at launch (and again if an Early Access title exits EA to full release), for discounts of 20%+ that hit the lowest-priced package for 8+ hours, and for one developer-controlled demo-release notification within two weeks of the demo going live (Valve, Steamworks Documentation, "Wishlists," https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/marketing/wishlist, accessed 2026). There's a **2-week cooldown** per customer per app between notifications (extended further during high-traffic sale periods), and Valve's own documentation explicitly states "there's no formula to accurately predict sales from wishlists" despite wishlist count being a commonly used proxy for interest.
- **Practical synthesis**: these two primary-source mechanics are the literal, mechanical reason community-management cadence (regular Steam news/announcement posts) matters beyond just "keeping fans happy" — a stale announcements feed directly disqualifies a studio from using Update Visibility Rounds (the 30-day recency requirement), and each of the five allocated rounds is a finite, valuable resource best spent on real content-worthy updates (new DLC, major patches, new features) rather than burned early on minor news.

## 3. DLC/edition economics for small games

### Documented attach rates and pricing from named small/indie titles (GameDiscoverCo, 2020)
The most concrete, independently checkable numbers found in this research pass come from GameDiscoverCo founder Simon Carless, citing real named games with real figures (Simon Carless, "The surprising way that paid DLC works," GameDiscoverCo newsletter, published July 13, 2020, https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/the-surprising-way-that-paid-dlc):
- An earlier (2017-era) industry benchmark cited in the piece: **5%–15% lifetime DLC attach rates** were considered typical at that time.
- **X-Morph: Defense**: 33% lifetime DLC attach rate; roughly 50% of concurrent sales were the bundled "Complete Edition"; attach rate rose to 60% during a 75%-off sale. Base game priced $19.99; Complete Edition (base + all DLC) priced $35.95 — roughly a 10% discount versus buying everything separately.
- **Hacknet**: DLC/soundtrack revenue amounted to roughly one-third of total revenue, versus two-thirds from the base game alone.
- **Not Tonight**: 21% DLC attach rate at full price, rising to 36% during a Steam Summer Sale (both game and DLC discounted 50%). Base game $19.99; story-expansion DLC priced at $9.99 (roughly half the base price).
- **Dead in Vinland**: three individual DLC packs saw 39%, 25%, and 28% attach rates respectively; combined DLC revenue equaled 14% of the game's lifetime revenue. Base game $19.99; DLC priced at $1.99 and $4.99 (i.e., DLC priced at roughly 10%–25% of the base game price in this case).
- Carless's strategic framing: DLC works best as "a long tail extension" that helps sustain post-launch discovery, rather than a launch-window event — his advice is to release DLC relatively soon after the base game (to catch it while there's still an active audience) and to lean on discounted "Complete Edition" bundles during sale events, since that's where attach rates spike hardest in his examples above.

### More recent, larger-sample tier analysis (howtomarketagame.com, 2026)
A newer analysis segments games by base-game gross revenue into four tiers and reports DLC prevalence and attach rates per tier, based on a survey of 52 games/83 DLC items plus a broader dataset of 14,399 games from 2024 (howtomarketagame.com, "Benchmark: How much money can you make from DLC?", by-line "Zukalous," published March 3, 2026, https://howtomarketagame.com/2026/03/03/benchmark-how-much-money-can-you-make-from-dlc/):
- **Revenue tiers**: Bronze ($0–$10K base game revenue), Silver ($10K–$149K), Gold ($150K–$999K), Diamond ($1M+).
- **Suggested threshold for DLC to make sense**: base games earning **$10K+** should consider a lightweight "supporter pack" style DLC at launch; games reaching **$150K+** should actively plan proper DLC.
- **DLC prevalence by tier (2024 dataset)**: only 68% of Diamond-tier games had any DLC at all; 47% of Gold-tier; 21% of Silver-tier — i.e., even among the most successful indie/small titles, roughly a third skip DLC entirely, and prevalence drops sharply as base-game revenue drops.
- **Attach rates and revenue**: Diamond-tier games averaged **$1,069,000** in combined DLC revenue across all DLC types; expansion-pack DLC specifically had a median of **$1,250,000** at that tier. Attach-rate figures varied widely and unevenly by DLC type and tier (e.g., Silver-tier "expansion" attach rate reported at 35.9% vs. Diamond-tier at 14%), suggesting attach rate alone is a noisy metric without controlling for DLC type.
- **Development time**: a lightweight "supporter pack" style DLC is reported to typically take "no more than a week or two" of dev time, making it a low-risk way to test whether an audience will pay for additional content before committing to a full expansion.
- **Illustrative extreme-DLC-count example**: Train Sim World 5 is cited as having 129 separately priced DLC items ($29.99–$39.99 each) while maintaining "very positive" review status (1,138 reviews) — offered as a counterexample to the assumption that heavy DLC volume automatically damages player sentiment, though Train Sim World is a niche simulation title with an audience that specifically wants purchasable route/train content, so this may not generalize to other genres.

### Season pass viability
This research pass did **not** find a specific, well-sourced, indie-scale season-pass viability figure or documented postmortem — most season-pass discussion found in searches was either AAA-scale (not applicable to indie budgets) or repeated in low-confidence content-mill sources not worth citing here. Reasoned from the DLC data above: since even Diamond-tier ($1M+ base revenue) indie titles see only ~68% DLC adoption and highly variable attach rates by DLC type, a season pass (which requires committing to a schedule of *future*, not-yet-built content sold upfront) is a materially higher-commitment bet than the single supporter-pack/expansion patterns documented above — this is inference from the surrounding data, not a directly sourced claim, and is flagged as a gap below.

### Deluxe/special edition bundling
The X-Morph: Defense "Complete Edition" example above (base + all DLC bundled at a ~10% discount versus buying separately, per Carless/GameDiscoverCo, July 13, 2020) is the clearest documented small-scale example of deluxe/complete-edition bundling economics found in this pass. No additional distinct sources specifically on "deluxe edition" (as opposed to "complete edition" bundling of existing DLC) pricing conventions for small games were found and independently verified in this research pass — flagged as a gap below.

### Synthesis: when the sourced data suggests DLC is/isn't worth it
Cross-referencing the two datasets above (Carless/GameDiscoverCo 2020 named-game examples; howtomarketagame.com 2026 tier survey) against each other, rather than treating either in isolation:
- Both sources treat DLC as a **long-tail revenue extension**, not a launch-window event — Carless explicitly frames it that way, and the howtomarketagame.com tier data (where Diamond-tier games, i.e., the ones that have had the most time and audience to accumulate DLC revenue, show the highest DLC prevalence) is consistent with that framing.
- Both sources converge on treating a lightweight/cheap DLC (soundtrack, supporter pack, cosmetic pack) as the low-risk first move — cheap to build (per howtomarketagame.com, "no more than a week or two"), and priced as a small fraction of the base game (per Carless's Dead in Vinland example, DLC priced at roughly 10%–25% of the $19.99 base) — before committing to a full paid expansion, which carries more development risk and (per the tier data) far more variable, less predictable attach rates.
- Neither source supports a single "magic number" attach rate to plan around — reported attach rates for comparable DLC types range roughly from the low-20%s to the high-30%s across the named 2020 examples, and vary further by tier and DLC type in the 2026 survey — so a business plan that assumes a specific attach rate without a comparable-game benchmark is building on an unsupported number.
