# Academic Thread: Procedural Rhetoric

## Evidence status

Bogost’s definition, date, book structure, and scope are **verified** against MIT Press and his own retrospective explanation. Sicart’s critique is **verified** against the full *Game Studies* article. Design procedures below are **synthesized**.

## Core thesis

- Procedural rhetoric is argumentation through process and model: software and games can represent how systems work by executing rules rather than only by describing them in words or images. (*Persuasive Games: The Expressive Power of Videogames*, Ian Bogost, 2007.)
- Bogost connects this rhetoric to procedurality, the computer’s capacity to execute rule-based symbolic processes, and argues that games can invite players to interact with a model and form judgments about it. (*Persuasive Games*, Bogost, 2007.)
- The original book applies the theory across politics, advertising, and learning, treating games as potentially expressive and persuasive rather than as neutral containers for messages. (*Persuasive Games*, Bogost, 2007.)

## Analytical grammar

- **Domain:** identify the real, imagined, or conceptual system the game models. (*Persuasive Games*, Bogost, 2007; synthesized method.)
- **Variables:** identify what the model counts, ignores, quantifies, or makes mutable. (*Persuasive Games*, Bogost, 2007; synthesized method.)
- **Procedures:** identify the causal rules that transform state and the actor allowed to trigger them. (*Persuasive Games*, Bogost, 2007; synthesized method.)
- **Goals and valuation:** identify which outcomes count as success, failure, efficiency, progress, or waste. (*Persuasive Games*, Bogost, 2007; synthesized method.)
- **Possibility space:** identify actions and outcomes the rules permit, forbid, or render unthinkable. (*Persuasive Games*, Bogost, 2007; synthesized method.)
- **Encounter:** identify what the player must do repeatedly to understand or contest the model, not merely what an explanatory text says. (*The Rhetoric of Video Games*, Ian Bogost, 2008; synthesized method.)
- **Gap:** compare the simulation with its source domain, because omissions and distortions can be rhetorically significant. (*Persuasive Games*, Bogost, 2007; synthesized method.)

## From claim to mechanic

The following is **synthesized content**, not a named Bogost workflow.

1. Write a contestable causal claim about the subject rather than a theme word. (*Persuasive Games*, Bogost, 2007; synthesized workflow.)
2. Choose state variables that make that causal claim operational. (*Persuasive Games*, Bogost, 2007; synthesized workflow.)
3. Implement rules whose repeated consequences let players test the claim. (*Persuasive Games*, Bogost, 2007; synthesized workflow.)
4. Give the player enough agency to probe or resist the model rather than only click through a predetermined proof. (*Persuasive Games*, Bogost, 2007; “Against Procedurality,” Miguel Sicart, 2011; synthesized workflow.)
5. Playtest interpretation as well as usability: ask what players think the system says, what they actually did, and what counter-readings emerged. (*Persuasive Games*, Bogost, 2007; “Against Procedurality,” Sicart, 2011; synthesized workflow.)

## Critique and correction

- Sicart argues that strong proceduralist readings can overprivilege designer-authored rules and understate the player’s embodied, personal, social, and appropriative production of meaning. (“Against Procedurality,” Miguel Sicart, 2011.)
- Sicart’s corrective is not to ignore systems but to pair procedural analysis with an orthogonal analysis of play, including rule breaking, reinterpretation, performance, and community. (“Against Procedurality,” Sicart, 2011.)
- Treanor and Mateas compare newsgames with political cartoons, suggesting that short-form persuasive games also depend on established rhetorical genres, audience context, and editorial framing rather than procedure alone. (“Newsgames: Procedural Rhetoric Meets Political Cartoons,” Mike Treanor and Michael Mateas, 2009; reconstructed summary.)
- A responsible reading therefore distinguishes **encoded model**, **performed play**, **player interpretation**, and **circulation context** rather than assigning a game one automatic message. (*Persuasive Games*, Bogost, 2007; “Against Procedurality,” Sicart, 2011; synthesized correction.)

## Connections to the design canon

- Salen and Zimmerman’s cultural-rhetoric schema is broader than procedural rhetoric because it includes representation, social practice, and cultural context as well as formal rules. (*Rules of Play*, Katie Salen Tekinbaş and Eric Zimmerman, 2003; *Persuasive Games*, Bogost, 2007; synthesized comparison.)
- Adams and Dormans’ “Meaningful Mechanics” makes a production-facing bridge from semiotic and communication theory to mechanics that model real-world claims. (*Game Mechanics*, Ernest Adams and Joris Dormans, 2012; *Persuasive Games*, Bogost, 2007; synthesized comparison.)
- Costikyan’s semiotic uncertainty explains why the intended procedural claim may not be the claim players perceive: the signs and operations themselves can remain ambiguous. (*Uncertainty in Games*, Greg Costikyan, 2013; *Persuasive Games*, Bogost, 2007; synthesized comparison.)
