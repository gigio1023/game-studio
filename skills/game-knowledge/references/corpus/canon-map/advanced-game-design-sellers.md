## Chapter/concept map

### Part I. Foundations

- Chapter 1, “Foundations of Systems,” introduces systems thinking as a way to understand wholes, relationships, boundaries, hierarchies, and behavior that cannot be read from isolated components. (*Advanced Game Design*, Sellers, 2017; reconstructed summary.)
- Chapter 2, “Defining Systems,” emphasizes that systems have interacting parts, boundaries, flows, feedback, state, and purpose; a mere list or one-way chain is not automatically a system. (*Advanced Game Design*, Sellers, 2017; reconstructed summary.)
- Chapter 3, “Foundations of Games and Game Design,” places games within systems theory and treats the designed artifact and active player as a coupled game-plus-player system. (*Advanced Game Design*, Sellers, 2017; reconstructed summary.)
- Chapter 4, “Interactivity and Fun,” defines interaction through mutual effect, then connects the player’s evolving mental model, arousal, and engagement to recursive interaction loops. (*Advanced Game Design*, Sellers, 2017; reconstructed summary.)

### Part II. Principles

- Chapter 5, “Working as a Systemic Game Designer,” shifts design attention from isolated features to relationships, feedback, second-order effects, and the whole experience. (*Advanced Game Design*, Sellers, 2017; reconstructed summary.)
- Chapter 6, “Designing the Whole Experience,” uses experience and design goals to align what the player perceives, does, learns, and feels across mechanics, fiction, interface, and production. (*Advanced Game Design*, Sellers, 2017; reconstructed summary.)
- Chapter 7, “Creating Game Loops,” treats loops as engines of interactive continuity: player action changes the game, the game returns perceivable state, and that state changes subsequent player action. (*Advanced Game Design*, Sellers, 2017; reconstructed summary.)
- Chapter 8, “Defining Game Parts,” identifies the tokens, relationships, attributes, resources, and transformations needed to instantiate those loops without losing sight of the whole. (*Advanced Game Design*, Sellers, 2017; reconstructed summary.)

### Part III. Practice

- Chapter 9, “Game Balance Methods,” introduces quantitative, qualitative, symmetry, cost-curve, feedback, and simulation-oriented approaches to balancing relationships. (*Advanced Game Design*, Sellers, 2017; reconstructed summary.)
- Chapter 10, “Game Balance Practice,” applies those methods iteratively because balance is experienced dynamically and cannot be proven from a spreadsheet alone. (*Advanced Game Design*, Sellers, 2017; reconstructed summary.)
- Chapter 11, “Working as a Team,” extends systems thinking to multidisciplinary development: communication structures and incentives affect the game that the team can perceive and build. (*Advanced Game Design*, Sellers, 2017; reconstructed summary.)
- Chapter 12, “Making Your Game Real,” moves from systemic concept to implementable artifact through prototypes, concept documents, scope decisions, iteration, and production constraints. (*Advanced Game Design*, Sellers, 2017; reconstructed summary.)

## Systems vocabulary

- **Boundary:** the analytical decision about what belongs to the system; moving it from “the rules” to “game plus player” changes the questions that can be answered. (*Advanced Game Design*, Sellers, 2017; reconstructed summary.)
- **Feedback loop:** a circular causal structure in which an effect returns to influence later causes; reinforcing and balancing loops yield different trajectories. (*Advanced Game Design*, Sellers, 2017; reconstructed summary.)
- **Emergence:** system-level behavior arising from interactions among parts rather than from a single scripted component. (*Advanced Game Design*, Sellers, 2017; reconstructed summary.)
- **Mental model:** the player’s inferred model of how the game works, revised through the action-feedback loop. (*Advanced Game Design*, Sellers, 2017; reconstructed summary.)
- **Holism:** local quality cannot guarantee whole-experience quality because parts can be individually polished yet relationally incoherent. (*Advanced Game Design*, Sellers, 2017; reconstructed summary.)

## Canonical contribution

- Sellers makes the strongest explicit case in this corpus for treating the player as part of the operative system rather than as an external consumer of a closed machine. (*Advanced Game Design*, Sellers, 2017; reconstructed synthesis.)
- The approach complements, but does not duplicate, MDA: MDA traces mechanics through runtime behavior to experience, whereas Sellers foregrounds circular causality, mental models, boundaries, and hierarchical systems. (*Advanced Game Design*, Sellers, 2017; *MDA*, Robin Hunicke, Marc LeBlanc, and Robert Zubek, 2004; reconstructed synthesis.)
