# Uncertainty in Games

## Evidence status

Author, 2013 publication date, seven-chapter structure, central thesis, and named uncertainty types are **verified** against MIT Press, Google Books, and follow-up research that reproduces the taxonomy. Explanations are **reconstructed summaries**.

## Bibliographic record

*Uncertainty in Games* is by Greg Costikyan, published by MIT Press on 2013-03-08, ISBN 9780262018968. (*Uncertainty in Games*, Greg Costikyan, 2013.)

## Chapter/concept map

- Chapter 1 introduces uncertainty as a central source of game interest and the struggle to master or manage it as a central pleasure. (*Uncertainty in Games*, Costikyan, 2013; publisher-verified summary.)
- Chapter 2 places games within culture as bounded constructions that let people encounter consequential uncertainty under comparatively safe, fictive conditions. (*Uncertainty in Games*, Costikyan, 2013; reconstructed summary.)
- Chapter 3 distinguishes uncertainty from randomness: uncertainty is the player’s lack of certainty about states, actions, meanings, or outcomes, and chance is only one possible cause. (*Uncertainty in Games*, Costikyan, 2013; reconstructed summary.)
- Chapter 4 analyzes games by locating what is uncertain, for whom, at what time, through what information, and by what means it can be managed. (*Uncertainty in Games*, Costikyan, 2013; reconstructed summary.)
- Chapter 5 supplies a taxonomy of sources rather than a single continuum of luck. (*Uncertainty in Games*, Costikyan, 2013; reconstructed summary.)
- Chapter 6 asks designers to combine, tune, reveal, and retire uncertainty so that it sustains decisions without making action unintelligible or outcomes meaningless. (*Uncertainty in Games*, Costikyan, 2013; reconstructed summary.)
- Chapter 7 concludes that uncertainty is a powerful but partial lens, not a complete theory of everything valuable in games. (*Uncertainty in Games*, Costikyan, 2013; reconstructed summary.)

## Eleven sources of uncertainty

1. **Performative uncertainty:** whether the player can execute the required physical or timed action. (*Uncertainty in Games*, Costikyan, 2013; reconstructed summary.)
2. **Solver’s uncertainty:** whether the player can discover a solution to a puzzle or problem. (*Uncertainty in Games*, Costikyan, 2013; reconstructed summary.)
3. **Player unpredictability:** what another human or human-like opponent will choose. (*Uncertainty in Games*, Costikyan, 2013; reconstructed summary.)
4. **Randomness:** outcomes produced by stochastic processes such as shuffled cards, dice, or random-number generators. (*Uncertainty in Games*, Costikyan, 2013; reconstructed summary.)
5. **Analytic complexity:** inability to calculate the best action because the state space, interactions, or lookahead exceed practical reasoning. (*Uncertainty in Games*, Costikyan, 2013; reconstructed summary.)
6. **Hidden information:** strategically unavailable state, such as concealed hands, fog of war, or unknown maps. (*Uncertainty in Games*, Costikyan, 2013; reconstructed summary.)
7. **Narrative anticipation:** uncertainty about what will happen in the story or authored sequence. (*Uncertainty in Games*, Costikyan, 2013; reconstructed summary.)
8. **Development anticipation:** uncertainty about later abilities, content, expansions, updates, or the future form of an ongoing game. (*Uncertainty in Games*, Costikyan, 2013; reconstructed summary.)
9. **Schedule uncertainty:** uncertainty tied to real-time availability, waiting periods, event timing, or when play opportunities occur. (*Uncertainty in Games*, Costikyan, 2013; reconstructed summary.)
10. **Uncertainty of perception:** difficulty detecting or parsing relevant sensory state. (*Uncertainty in Games*, Costikyan, 2013; reconstructed summary.)
11. **Semiotic uncertainty:** uncertainty about what signs, actions, objects, or representations mean within the game. (*Uncertainty in Games*, Costikyan, 2013; reconstructed summary.)

## Design operations

- **Locate:** identify the source rather than describing a game vaguely as “random” or “unpredictable.” (*Uncertainty in Games*, Costikyan, 2013; reconstructed synthesis.)
- **Assign:** specify which player or audience lacks the information; asymmetric uncertainty can create bluffing, suspense, or unfairness. (*Uncertainty in Games*, Costikyan, 2013; reconstructed synthesis.)
- **Time:** decide when uncertainty appears and when it resolves; anticipation, surprise, and planning require different reveal schedules. (*Uncertainty in Games*, Costikyan, 2013; reconstructed synthesis.)
- **Make manageable:** give players actions, clues, probabilities, redundancy, practice, or hedges so uncertainty becomes material for agency. (*Uncertainty in Games*, Costikyan, 2013; reconstructed synthesis.)
- **Combine carefully:** a novel mixture can create a genre-defining experience, while too many simultaneous unknowns can destroy the player’s causal model. (*Uncertainty in Games*, Costikyan, 2013; reconstructed synthesis.)

## Canonical contribution

- Costikyan’s decisive contribution is separating uncertainty from chance, allowing dexterity, opponents, complexity, hidden state, story, live development, timing, perception, and interpretation to be compared within one design lens. (*Uncertainty in Games*, Costikyan, 2013; reconstructed synthesis.)
- The taxonomy complements flow and learning accounts: challenge can remain engaging only while uncertainty is both unresolved and tractable enough for skilled action. (*Uncertainty in Games*, Costikyan, 2013; *Flow*, Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, 1990; reconstructed synthesis.)
