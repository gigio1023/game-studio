## Characteristic map

### 1. Basics

- **Length of playtime** distinguishes useful scales such as atom, game, match, session, and campaign because “how long is the game?” changes with the unit and community practice. (*Characteristics of Games*, Elias, Garfield, and Gutschera, 2012; reconstructed summary.)
- **Number of players** changes the structure of opposition, cooperation, observation, downtime, politics, and scalability rather than simply increasing content. (*Characteristics of Games*, Elias, Garfield, and Gutschera, 2012; reconstructed summary.)
- **Heuristics** are learnable rules of thumb used to evaluate positions or choose directions; a rich heuristic tree gives players a path from novice competence toward mastery. (*Characteristics of Games*, Elias, Garfield, and Gutschera, 2012; reconstructed summary.)

### 2. Multiplayer Games

- **Player elimination** can raise stakes and simplify the remaining contest, but it also converts playtime into involuntary spectation and can impose a severe social cost. (*Characteristics of Games*, Elias, Garfield, and Gutschera, 2012; reconstructed summary.)
- **Interactivity** measures how much players affect one another; multiplayer races and multiplayer brawls occupy different regions even at the same player count. (*Characteristics of Games*, Elias, Garfield, and Gutschera, 2012; reconstructed summary.)
- **Politics** emerges when players can negotiate, threaten, ally, exchange, or coordinate outside fixed mechanical choices. (*Characteristics of Games*, Elias, Garfield, and Gutschera, 2012; reconstructed summary.)
- **Kingmaking** occurs when a player who cannot win can substantially determine which other player does, creating agency but also legitimacy problems. (*Characteristics of Games*, Elias, Garfield, and Gutschera, 2012; reconstructed summary.)
- **Teamwork** asks whether players share goals, information, roles, resources, and dependency in ways that reward coordination rather than parallel solo play. (*Characteristics of Games*, Elias, Garfield, and Gutschera, 2012; reconstructed summary.)

### 3. Infrastructure

- **Rules and standards** separate the game’s own prescriptions from reusable conventions that lower learning cost across a genre or culture. (*Characteristics of Games*, Elias, Garfield, and Gutschera, 2012; reconstructed summary.)
- **Outcomes and ending conditions** distinguish winning, losing, ranking, scoring, self-chosen goals, and the event that actually stops play. (*Characteristics of Games*, Elias, Garfield, and Gutschera, 2012; reconstructed summary.)
- **Positional asymmetry** covers unequal starts, roles, powers, information, or turn order and asks whether difference is fair, compensated, rotated, or intentionally dramatic. (*Characteristics of Games*, Elias, Garfield, and Gutschera, 2012; reconstructed summary.)
- **Sensory feedback** must communicate game state and consequence while also supporting pleasure, theme, and accessibility. (*Characteristics of Games*, Elias, Garfield, and Gutschera, 2012; reconstructed summary.)

### 4. Games as Systems

- **Abstract subgames and the essential game** separate strategically meaningful decision structures from procedures that can be removed without changing the central contest. (*Characteristics of Games*, Elias, Garfield, and Gutschera, 2012; reconstructed summary.)
- **Snowball and catch-up** describe feedback that amplifies or reduces advantage; both affect whether early success matters and whether late decisions remain consequential. (*Characteristics of Games*, Elias, Garfield, and Gutschera, 2012; reconstructed summary.)
- **Complexity-tree growth and game arc** describe how the branching of possible decisions expands or contracts over time, shaping pacing and cognitive load. (*Characteristics of Games*, Elias, Garfield, and Gutschera, 2012; reconstructed summary.)
- **Game balance and strategic collapse** distinguish a diverse strategic landscape from one in which solved or dominant choices eliminate meaningful alternatives. (*Characteristics of Games*, Elias, Garfield, and Gutschera, 2012; reconstructed summary.)

### 5. Indeterminacy

- **Randomness** is one source of unpredictability whose placement, visibility, frequency, and magnitude matter more than the mere presence of dice or random numbers. (*Characteristics of Games*, Elias, Garfield, and Gutschera, 2012; reconstructed summary.)
- **Luck and skill** are not strict opposites: short-term results, long-run mastery, opponent matching, and opportunities to mitigate chance all alter the perceived mix. (*Characteristics of Games*, Elias, Garfield, and Gutschera, 2012; reconstructed summary.)
- **Hidden information** creates inference and risk when information is strategically withheld, but creates confusion when players cannot build a useful model. (*Characteristics of Games*, Elias, Garfield, and Gutschera, 2012; reconstructed summary.)

### 6. Player Effort

- **Costs** include money, setup, learning, scheduling, social exposure, attention, dexterity, and hardware, not just in-game resource expenditure. (*Characteristics of Games*, Elias, Garfield, and Gutschera, 2012; reconstructed summary.)
- **Rewards** include outcomes, mastery, status, expression, discovery, sensation, and social experience; their value differs by player and community. (*Characteristics of Games*, Elias, Garfield, and Gutschera, 2012; reconstructed summary.)
- **Downtime and busywork** consume player effort without supplying proportionate agency or interest, although observation or routine can be meaningful in the right social and systemic context. (*Characteristics of Games*, Elias, Garfield, and Gutschera, 2012; reconstructed summary.)
- **Reward/effort ratio** is a diagnostic relationship rather than a demand for constant reward: demanding effort can be valuable when it produces commensurate learning, tension, expression, or achievement. (*Characteristics of Games*, Elias, Garfield, and Gutschera, 2012; reconstructed summary.)

### 7. Superstructure

- **Metagame** includes preparation, community knowledge, tournament structure, collection, status, and strategy outside the bounded act of play. (*Characteristics of Games*, Elias, Garfield, and Gutschera, 2012; reconstructed summary.)
- **Conceit/motif** supplies the representational frame through which abstract procedures become intelligible, memorable, or emotionally charged. (*Characteristics of Games*, Elias, Garfield, and Gutschera, 2012; reconstructed summary.)
- **Spectation, customization, and misbehavior** show that audiences, player-authored variation, and norm violation are structural parts of a game’s life rather than peripheral noise. (*Characteristics of Games*, Elias, Garfield, and Gutschera, 2012; reconstructed summary.)
- **Play lifetime** asks how long a game remains worth learning, replaying, discussing, maintaining, or evolving for individuals and communities. (*Characteristics of Games*, Elias, Garfield, and Gutschera, 2012; reconstructed summary.)

## Canonical contribution

- The book’s method is comparative and multidimensional: games are located along characteristics rather than sorted into a single taxonomy of genres. (*Characteristics of Games*, Elias, Garfield, and Gutschera, 2012; reconstructed synthesis.)
- Its player-centered vocabulary makes design tradeoffs explicit: a feature that improves one characteristic, such as high interactivity, may worsen another, such as downtime or political volatility. (*Characteristics of Games*, Elias, Garfield, and Gutschera, 2012; reconstructed synthesis.)
