# Pitch & Marketing Talks

Talks about getting a game funded, published, or discovered — pitching to
publishers, and marketing directly to players on Steam. Distinct from the
design/craft lanes: the audience for these talks is "how do I get this
game seen/funded," not "how do I make it better."

---

## Thirty Things I Hate About Your Game Pitch

- **Speaker:** Brian Upton (veteran designer — prior credits include Sony
  Santa Monica; at the time of this talk, publisher-side at "Game On The
  Rails")
- **Venue / year:** GDC 2017
- **URLs:** GDC Vault — https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1023998/Thirty-Things-I-Hate-About
  · Slides (PDF, binary — not machine-readable via automated fetch) —
  https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2017/Presentations/Upton_Brian_Thirty%20Things%20I.pdf
  · YouTube — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4LTtr45y7P0
- **Speaker verification note:** The research brief flagged this title as
  needing speaker verification, listing "Brent Fox?" and "Ed Fries?" as
  candidates to check. **Confirmed: neither.** The speaker is **Brian
  Upton**. This is corroborated by the GDC Vault listing, the official
  slide filename (`Upton_Brian_Thirty Things I.pdf`), and the Xsolla/
  Boing Boing/ResetEra coverage, which all independently attribute it to
  Upton, described as a veteran designer who had by that point heard
  hundreds of pitches from the publisher side.
- **Verification status:** Summary-based on a detailed third-party recap
  (Medium/Fastrecap) that appears to walk the full slide deck point by
  point; not a primary transcript, but comprehensive enough to be treated

### Core claims — the 30 (+ bonus) items, grouped

**Content/framing mistakes:**
1. Including material irrelevant to whether the game is worth making or
   the team can make it.
2. Over-emphasizing backstory — "the backstory of the game is not
   important"; keep context brief.
3. Explaining routine/standard features (e.g., a normal inventory system)
   instead of what's actually special.
4. Asking the publisher questions instead of proposing confident answers
   — "publishers won't design your game for you."
5. No clear hook distinguishing the game from what already exists.
6. Spending pitch time on world-building/story/monetization instead of
   actual gameplay and player experience.
7. Using "realism" as a justification for bad design choices in a
   non-simulation game.
8. Adding an unnecessary frame story around a mechanic that's already
   compelling on its own.
9. Pre-emptively labeling the game "a parody," which raises viability
   doubts rather than lowering them.

**Technical/production credibility mistakes:**
10. Underestimating or being unprepared for real technical risk.
11. Demonstrating the easy parts of a prototype instead of the genuinely
    hard/novel parts.
12. Showing many mediocre art assets instead of "one or two great" ones.
13. Not clearly marking what's placeholder vs. final.
14. Polishing visuals before core mechanics are proven to work.
15. Weak dialogue/writing quality in a game where story is central.
16. Using trendy tech (VR/AR) because it's trendy, not because it serves
    the game.

**Business-fit mistakes:**
17. Pitching to a publisher whose portfolio doesn't match the game's
    genre or platform.
18. Presenting a clone without explaining why it beats the original.
19. Unresolved IP/licensing issues, expecting the publisher to sort them
    out.
20. Going deep into elaborate free-to-play monetization schemes
    prematurely.
21. Unrealistic scope estimates — not understanding required team size,
    budget, timeline, content volume, or expected playtime.
22. An incomplete team with no hiring plan or line on qualified people.
23. Inflated projections (his example: expecting to match World of
    Warcraft's numbers).

**Interpersonal / meeting-conduct mistakes:**
24. Being disagreeable — a reminder that this may be a relationship
    lasting "6 months... maybe 18 months or years."
25. Assuming the publisher already knows who you are; provide background
    if you're not a known quantity.
26. Getting defensive or going off-script when asked clarifying
    questions.
27. Presenting on a phone screen instead of a tablet or laptop.
28. Using earbuds instead of proper headphones in a noisy room.
29. Being visibly impaired (drunk/high/hungover) in the meeting.
30. Badmouthing other companies or people, which reads as a
    professionalism red flag.

**Bonus items called out separately:** poor personal/device hygiene,
visible lack of enthusiasm for your own project, and dishonesty about
your experience or the challenges you've actually faced.

### What problem it solves for a solo dev

It's a pre-flight checklist for the one or two pitch meetings a solo dev
might actually get, written from the buyer's side of the table — useful
specifically because most of the items aren't about the game's quality at
all, they're about wasted trust (overclaiming, underprepared logistics,
mismatched targeting) that kills a pitch regardless of how good the game
is.

## No Time, No Budget, No Problem: Finishing 'The First Tree'

- **Speaker:** David Wehle (solo developer, background in video
  editing/animation)
- **Venue / year:** GDC 2019, March 19
- **URLs:** GDC Vault — https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1025702/No-Time-No-Budget-No
  · YouTube — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g5f7yixtQPc
- **Verification status:** Summary-based on a detailed third-party recap
  from a specialist marketing blog (howtomarketagame.com); numbers below
  are consistent across that source and general press coverage, but not
  confirmed against a primary transcript.

### Core claims — numbers and tactics

**Constraints.** Wehle built *The First Tree* while working 40+ hours/
week at a separate job (The VOID) and raising two kids with his wife —
the talk is framed explicitly around finishing under real-world time
scarcity, not idealized full-time solo dev conditions.

- **Budget:** ~$10,000 total (assets + trade-show travel).
- **Time:** ~18 months of part-time work, roughly 1,000 total hours
  (~10 hours/week).
- **Launch date:** September 13, 2017.
- **Revenue:** ~$150,000 from the PC launch, plus another ~$150,000 from
  subsequent console ports.

**Production shortcuts.** Bought pre-made assets from Pond5, TurboSquid,
Freesound, MusicBed, and Envato — estimated to have saved 600+ hours of
work he'd otherwise have had to do himself. He explicitly embraced a
"compromised vision": kept the game short by design rather than chasing
feature-completeness, treating shipping over scope as the deliberate
tradeoff.

**Marketing tactic — GIF-first social posting.** Core method: "make GIF,
post GIF, rinse, repeat," sustained daily for roughly 12 months across
social channels, using `#madewithunity` and similar community hashtags.
Roughly a third of his eventual Twitter following is attributed to
community retweets from that hashtag exposure. GIFs are cited as
outperforming static images by roughly **10x** in engagement.

**Launch-day sequencing.** Coordinated email list + Steam wishlist +
social activity as a synchronized push to try to land in Steam's "New and
Trending" section. Specific claim: email was the *most* impactful channel
in the first hour of launch, driving the initial wave of Steam traffic
that then fed into organic Reddit momentum afterward — i.e., the email
list was used to manufacture the initial spike that the platform's own
discovery algorithms then amplified.

**Platform contrast.** Steam's discovery is algorithm-driven; console
storefronts (his example: Nintendo) were more relationship-driven,
featuring his game directly through personal contact rather than an
automated system.

### What problem it solves for a solo dev

A concrete existence proof that a part-time, low-budget, "compromised"
scope can still ship and earn real money, plus two specific, repeatable
tactics: (1) sustained daily GIF posting as a cheap attention-generation
loop, and (2) treating email list size as the actual lever for launch-day
discovery-algorithm placement, rather than treating wishlists or social
followers as sufficient on their own.

## Chris Zukowski — Steam Marketing / Wishlist Talks

Zukowski (author of *How to Market a Game*, howtomarketagame.com) has
given several related GDC sessions rather than one single named talk;
treating them as one cluster here since they share a thesis and speaker.
Titles found: "Marketing Your Game Under the New Steam Rules"
(Independent Games Summit), "30 Minute Steam Page Makeovers," and "The
Steam Secrets Nobody Tells You... Until Now" (GDC 2024).

- **Verification status:** Summary-based on detailed third-party recaps of
  the GDC 2024 talk specifically; not a primary transcript.

### Core claims — the GDC 2024 talk ("The Steam Secrets Nobody Tells You")

**Format note:** 143 slides in a 30-minute slot, per Zukowski's own
framing — signals this is a rapid-fire tactics dump, not a single deep
narrative.

**Central thesis, in his own words:** "The secret to Steam is asking for
shit" — i.e., Valve is unexpectedly responsive to specific, logistically
reasonable developer requests (for featuring, bundling, festival slots),
but developers routinely leave that value unclaimed by never asking.

**Four secrets, as summarized in coverage:**
1. Steam itself generates more visibility for a game than anything a
   developer can independently produce through press or social media.
2. There are effectively **two separate Steam stores** (the implication
   being personalized/algorithmic discovery vs. the manually curated
   surfaces — the exact split wasn't recoverable from available sources,
   flagged as a gap below).
3. Steam is a curated platform "but only if you ask for it" — curation
   opportunities aren't automatic.
4. The **Support page** is characterized as the single most important
   page on a developer's Steam presence.

**Specific numbers:**
- **Wishlist velocity for "Top Upcoming" placement:** roughly **1,000
  wishlists/day** needed to land there.
- **Total wishlist range to reliably land in the "Top Ten" of Top
  Upcoming:** roughly **5,000-7,000**, with the caveat that timing (when
  you hit that number relative to the list's refresh cycle) matters more
  than the raw ranking among the ten.
- **"Golden goose games"** — his term for a healthy commercial outcome:
  clearing **$150,000 in sales within 6 months of release**, generally
  paired with **around 500 user reviews**.
- **"Chocolate Tier"** — Valve reportedly sends a $300 luxury chocolate
  box to developers earning over **$1 million/year** on Steam; presented
  as an aspirational marker, not a realistic target for most developers,
  used rhetorically to frame how few developers reach that tier.
- **Market growth context:** from 2019 to 2024, annual Steam releases grew
  substantially, reaching **over 18,000 games released in 2024** — used
  to argue that discoverability tactics matter more every year as the
  catalog grows.

**Localization priority order (stated explicitly):** Simplified Chinese
first — his claim is that Simplified Chinese has overtaken English as the
most-spoken language on Steam, and that translation cost there is "worth
every penny" — then Japanese, then Korean, then the FIGS bundle (French,
Italian, German, Spanish).

**Tactical framing throughout:** because Valve takes a 30% cut regardless,
his repeated advice is to actively extract value from that relationship
(featuring requests, bundle placement, festival inclusion) rather than
passively accepting whatever the algorithm surfaces — "make them earn
that 30%."

### What problem it solves for a solo dev

Converts "Steam marketing" from a vague anxiety into a small number of
concrete, checkable numbers: a daily wishlist velocity target, a total
wishlist range to aim for pre-launch, a revenue/review bar that defines
"worked," and a specific ordering for where translation budget should go
first. All of it assumes zero PR budget, which matches the solo/small-
team default case.

### Gap

The exact meaning of "two separate Steam stores" wasn't recoverable from
available sources and is worth a follow-up watch of the full 2024 talk if
GDC Vault access becomes available — the summary sources referenced it
without unpacking what the two stores actually are.
