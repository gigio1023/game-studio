# Genre Wisdom: Simulation/Management (Story Generators) and Idle/Incremental

Two genres unified by a shared design move: **the designer builds systems, not stories or content curves, and the player's experience is generated by those systems running rather than authored directly.** Simulation games generate emergent *narrative*; idle games generate an emergent *number curve* the player rides. Both fail in the same general way when the underlying systems are too simple to generate anything worth experiencing.

---

## Simulation/management: interlocking systems as story generators

### Core promise

The player's memorable moments come from **unscripted interactions between independent systems**, not from authored events — the designer's job is to build systems that are individually simple but that *collide* in ways that produce specific, personal, tellable stories.

### Source

Tynan Sylvester (creator of *RimWorld*) — cited framing: "the real engine of story is the gameplay systems themselves." Coverage: "How RimWorld fleshes out the Dwarf Fortress formula," https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-i-rimworld-i-fleshes-out-the-i-dwarf-fortress-i-formula ; "Rimworld, Dwarf Fortress, and procedurally generated storytelling," https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/rimworld-dwarf-fortress-and-procedurally-generated-story-telling ; "How Dwarf Fortress and Rimworld tell radically different stories," https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/dwarf-fortress-and-rimworld-tell-very-different-stories. **Note:** Sylvester's own book, *Designing Games: A Guide to Engineering Experiences* (2013), is the primary-source origin for much of this thinking about emergent narrative and was not directly re-verified in this pass — treat direct quotes attributed to it as needing verification against the original text; the framing above is drawn from secondary coverage that itself cites Sylvester.

### Canonical lineage: Dwarf Fortress → RimWorld

*Dwarf Fortress* (Bay 12 Games, 2006–ongoing) established the "story generator" category: a game whose fun comes from watching non-scripted stories emerge from player actions and simulated systems colliding, rather than from reaching authored objectives. *RimWorld* (Ludeon Studios, 2018) is explicitly built as a more accessible, more narratively-legible descendant of the same formula — the cited distinction between the two games ("radically different stories") is that RimWorld deliberately simplifies characterization relative to Dwarf Fortress's exhaustive simulation depth, in exchange for a **smaller cast the player can actually get to know individually**, plus explicit relationship generation (family ties, grudges, romances) layered on top of the simulation specifically to make mechanical interactions carry more legible emotional weight.

### The technique: systems that collide

The specific example repeatedly cited across sources: a colonist's pet dies → that colonist suffers a mental break (a simulated psychological system) → in their broken state they punch another colonist (a simulated social/combat system) → the punched colonist, who already held a grudge from an earlier unrelated incident, escalates the conflict (a simulated relationship-memory system) → a raid arrives at that exact moment (a simulated threat-scheduling system) → the colony's response is compromised by the ongoing internal conflict. No single one of these systems is complex or scripted — each is a simple, general-purpose simulation rule — but their **intersection**, driven by simulated timing coincidence, produces a specific, memorable, tellable story that no author wrote.

### The AI Storyteller as a distinct layer

RimWorld adds an explicit **AI Storyteller**: an algorithm that selects events from a categorized pool based on live statistics about colony state (population, wealth, recent event history), deliberately introducing randomized elements to keep the simulation from settling into a predictable rhythm. This is structurally the same technique as the *Left 4 Dead* AI Director covered in `dialogue-barks-and-drama-managers.md` — a runtime policy selecting from a content pool based on live state — applied to *macro* colony-level events (raids, disasters, visitors) rather than to moment-to-moment combat pacing. Worth naming directly: simulation-game "story generation" is not purely emergent from player-facing systems alone — it's typically emergent systems **plus** a deliberate external event-pacing layer that shapes when pressure is applied, mirroring the drama-manager pattern from the narrative-design cluster of this corpus.

### Design takeaway: agency plus unpredictability, not either alone

The sourced synthesis: the best non-scripted stories emerge specifically at the **intersection of player agency and unpredictability** — a simulation with total player control but no randomness generates only stories the player already intended (not surprising, not "emergent" in any interesting sense); a simulation with total randomness and no player agency generates surprising but unearned, unactionable noise. The design target is enough player freedom that outcomes feel authored-by-the-player's-choices, combined with enough systemic unpredictability (random events, simulated agents with independent drives) that outcomes aren't fully foreseeable at the moment of choice.

## Idle/incremental: number curve as the entire design

### Core promise

Distinct from every other genre in this corpus in that the primary design object is **a mathematical growth curve**, not a space, a story, or a moment-to-moment interaction loop — the player's felt sense of "progress" is a direct read of that curve's shape over session time.

### Source

"The Math of Idle Games, Part III," *Game Developer*, https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-math-of-idle-games-part-iii (part of a series on the underlying mathematics; only Part III surfaced directly in this research pass — Parts I and II likely cover foundational curve mechanics and should be sourced directly for a fuller treatment). General mechanics reference: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incremental_game.

### Core mechanics

- **Exponential resource growth** paired with **exponentially escalating costs** — the archetypal case (*Cookie Clicker*, Orteil, 2013) starts the player at one unit of currency per manual action, lets them buy a generator producing currency automatically, and prices every subsequent purchase (of the same generator or a new tier) at a cost that scales faster than the income it unlocks. The design intent, per the sourced framing: progress should *feel* like it's continuously accelerating even though the underlying ratio of cost-to-benefit is deliberately being tuned to approach a plateau — "until the next plateau" is the operative phrase; the game is a chain of plateaus each broken by the next unlock, not a single smooth curve.
- **Automated production ("idle" accumulation)** — resources continue accruing while the player is not actively engaged, which is the genre-defining mechanic that separates it from a pure clicker.
- **Prestige/reset loops** — a deliberate mechanic where the player voluntarily resets their accumulated progress in exchange for a smaller, permanent multiplier or unlock that makes the *next* playthrough of the same curve faster. This is the idle-genre's direct structural analog to roguelite meta-progression (see `genre-roguelike.md`): a designed incentive to restart from zero, made palatable by permanent carry-over power.
- **Numeral scaling/naming conventions** — as numbers exceed comfortable display range, incremental games adopt scientific notation, abbreviation schemes (K/M/B/T or similar), or invented naming schemes for extremely large numbers, purely as a UX necessity created by the genre's own math.

### A notable secondary effect worth preserving

The sourced coverage on *Cookie Clicker* specifically notes that as the in-game currency scales into truly enormous magnitudes, the game's own framing and tone shift toward **cosmic horror** — the sheer scale of the numbers being generated becomes, in itself, a source of tonal material (the absurdity/horror of a universe reorganized around cookie production). This is a specific, citable instance of a genre-wide opportunity: because the number curve *is* the core design object in this genre, its emotional register (triumphant, absurd, horrifying, meditative) is an available authorial lever most designers under-use — the curve doesn't have to be tonally neutral.

### Design takeaway

Idle-game design work is disproportionately about **curve-tuning**: choosing growth rates, cost-scaling ratios, and unlock-cadence such that a session (and a full playthrough) delivers a felt sense of continuous acceleration punctuated by satisfying plateau-breaks, rather than either a flat grind or an overwhelming, meaningless number-soup. This makes the genre unusually amenable to spreadsheet-first design and playtesting-via-simulation (modeling the curve mathematically before any content is built) in a way most other genres in this corpus are not.
