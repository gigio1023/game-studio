# Animal Well — Billy Basso

- Roughly 6–7 years of solo development, including a custom engine.
- Roughly 500 of about 750 designed encounters were cut — about a two-thirds cut rate.
- Basso discusses directly the moment he recognised he needed outside help with business and marketing.
- Rule: a solo developer cannot stay solo through launch; the skill is naming that moment before it is forced.
- Designed content volume is not shipped content volume. Budget the difference.
- Verification: full-episode developer interview (AIAS podcast, July 2024). No GDC talk or written postmortem.

## Basics

**The cut rate is the number to remember.** Around 750 encounters were designed;
around 250 shipped. Two-thirds of the design work did not reach players. This is
not presented as a failure or a scoping error — it is what selecting for quality
across a large designed set actually costs. A solo developer planning content
volume should assume the ratio between designed and shipped is substantially
less than one, and should plan the schedule around designing more than will
ship rather than around shipping everything designed.

**Six to seven years is the price of that approach.** The long timeline and the
high cut rate are the same fact seen twice. If the schedule cannot absorb
designing three things to ship one, then either the content set shrinks or the
selection bar drops.

**The help moment is a named skill.** Basso explicitly discusses recognising
that he needed outside help with the business and marketing side. The
transferable content is not "hire someone" — it is that the recognition itself
came as a distinct event that he had to notice and act on, and that solo
development does not extend cleanly through launch. Across the practitioner
sources gathered in the 2026-07-26 research pass, this recurs: solo developers
reliably hit a point where non-development work (business, marketing, QA)
exceeds what they can absorb, and the postmortems consistently flag recognising
that point as a skill in itself.

## Advanced

**Designing for a cut rate changes what you design.** If two-thirds of
encounters will be cut, then encounters must be cheap to author and cheap to
discard. A content pipeline where each item costs weeks cannot support this
ratio; one where items are composed from reusable systems can. The high cut rate
is only affordable because the marginal encounter was cheap.

**How to detect the help moment before it forces itself.** It arrives as
non-development work displacing development work, not as an emergency. Concrete
triggers worth watching, in the order they usually appear: a store page that
needs writing and screenshots, a demo build that needs its own QA pass, press
and creator outreach, and business or tax setup at first revenue. If the last
four weeks contain more of that work than gameplay work, the moment has already
passed.

**What this case does not license.** A six-to-seven-year solo build with a
custom engine is an unusual bet, not a template. Cite it for the cut-rate
discipline and the help-moment recognition; do not cite it as evidence that
long solo timelines are normal or safe. For the contrasting timeline, see
[a-short-hike.md](./a-short-hike.md) (roughly one quarter); for the case where
a long timeline is a deliberate, funded philosophy rather than a consequence,
see [dwarf-fortress.md](./dwarf-fortress.md).

## Checklist

- [ ] How many pieces of content have you designed, and how many have shipped?
      If the ratio is near 1, your selection bar is probably the thing that is
      missing.
- [ ] What does one unit of content cost to author? Can you afford to throw
      away two of every three?
- [ ] In the last four weeks, was more of your time development or
      non-development work? If non-development, the help moment has arrived.
- [ ] Who does the store page, the demo QA, the outreach, and the business
      setup? If the answer to all four is "me, later", name a date.
