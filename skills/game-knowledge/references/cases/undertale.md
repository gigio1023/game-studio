# Undertale — ship smaller than your ambition

- Rule: make the first game smaller than the game you actually want to make.
- The Undertale-specific claim (deliberately scoped below the ambition that became Deltarune) is **unverified** — no primary developer source was located.
- The rule itself is carried by three named sources that do not depend on Undertale.
- Derek Yu: after an abandoned project, scale *down*, not up; finishing is a trainable skill.
- A Short Hike: a larger project was abandoned first; the small one shipped in about a quarter.
- The First Tree: ~$10k budget, ~1,000 hours over ~18 months part-time, ~$150k PC revenue plus ~$150k from console ports.

## Basics

**Read the sourcing before using the case.** In the 2026-07-26 research pass, no
GDC talk, written postmortem, or directly quoted developer statement was found
for the claim that Toby Fox deliberately scoped Undertale below his real
ambition in order to earn the right to build the larger game later. What exists
is practitioner consensus aggregated across interview outlets. Treat the
Undertale framing as an illustration, not as evidence, and do not attribute the
reasoning to Fox in the creator's presence as though it were a quote.

**The rule survives without it.** Three named sources establish the same
discipline independently:

*Derek Yu, "Finishing a Game."* A viable idea has to pass three tests at once —
a game you want to make, a game you want to have made, and a game you are
actually equipped to make — plus the hard constraint of whether you have the
time and resources to finish it. After a project gets abandoned, the instinct to
make the next one bigger to compensate is named explicitly as the wrong move: go
smaller to rebuild the finishing habit. Finishing is framed as a trainable skill
whose reputation, confidence, and practical knowledge compound into every later
project.

*A Short Hike.* Robinson-Yu abandoned a larger project and shipped a small one
in roughly a quarter, with a core-versus-stretch scope split fixed before
production. See [a-short-hike.md](./a-short-hike.md).

*The First Tree.* David Wehle shipped an explicitly "compromised vision" — kept
short by design rather than chasing feature-completeness — on about $10,000 and
roughly 1,000 hours spread over about 18 months at ~10 hours/week, while working
40+ hours at another job. It earned roughly $150,000 on PC and about the same
again from console ports.

## Advanced

**What "smaller" means operationally.** Not "less ambitious in feeling" — the
reduction is in content volume, system count, and production surface, while the
distinctive bet stays intact. The First Tree kept its emotional thesis and cut
length. A Short Hike kept its world and cut features into post-launch updates.
The failure mode is the reverse: keeping the content scope and diluting what
makes the game distinct.

**Why this argument gets rejected.** Creators hear "make something smaller" as
"make something lesser," and the honest counter is the compounding argument, not
the risk argument: shipping a small game buys the audience, the tools, the
finishing habit, and the credibility that make the large game possible. The
large game does not become unbuildable — it becomes the second one.

**Related discipline: defer, do not fold in.** Yu's rule for good ideas arriving
mid-project is to write them down for the *next* project rather than absorb them
into the current one. That is the mechanism that keeps a scoped-down first game
from re-inflating.

**Where the evidence is genuinely thin.** The claim that scoping down
*specifically causes* commercial success is not established by any source here.
The First Tree's revenue figures are the only concrete commercial numbers, from
a summary-based recap of a GDC talk. The supported claim is narrower: scoping
below your ambition raises the probability of finishing, and finishing is what
compounds.

## Checklist

- [ ] Name the game you actually want to make. Is the current project smaller
      than it? By what — content volume, system count, or both?
- [ ] Does the smaller version keep the distinctive bet, or did it dilute the
      bet and keep the content?
- [ ] Is there a written "next project" list capturing ideas arriving now?
- [ ] Has a previous project been abandoned? If so, is this one smaller than
      that one, or bigger?
- [ ] If quoting Undertale in a conversation, are you flagging it as
      unverified consensus rather than a developer statement?
